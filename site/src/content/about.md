# About this book

Old Wives is a field guide to creatures from folk belief. This first volume covers ten
Slavic creatures as a pilot. Each clause of lore in an entry is tagged with its source
and page, and every entry has an attestation grade that says how well the
creature is attested.

## What is in, and what is out

One test decides every entry: is anyone alive today supposed to be praying to it? If
yes, it is out. If no, it is in.

- **In:** folk creatures and spirits, monsters from epics and tales, and beings that
  were feared, avoided or appeased rather than worshipped.
- **Out:** anything in a living religion's canon or practice.

Appeasement is not worship. Milk left out for the domovoi is a way of managing fear,
not devotion, so the domovoi is in.

For the Slavic volume, two groups are excluded under this rule:

- the pre-Christian gods (Perun, Veles, Mokosh and the rest);
- all Rodnovery neopagan reconstructions.

## A kind of being and its named members

A kind of being can be in while a named, venerated member of that kind is out. The
zmey is an example: the class is in, with Zmey Gorynych as its best-known instance.
Neither has a cult, so both are in.

## The plates are drawings, not reconstructions

Each plate is a drawing after the folk tradition: how the creature has come to be
pictured. It is not a reconstruction from the sources. The plates were drawn from a
visual description, while the entries stay limited to what the sources say.

So that the two are never confused, every spread puts the sourced description beside
the plate, under a fixed line: *"A drawing after the folk tradition. The sources
describe:"*, followed by the appearance clauses from the entry with their citations.

## Peoples and old labels

Entries name specific peoples (Russians, Ukrainians, Serbs and so on), never just
"Slavic". The nineteenth-century sources often use labels that do not map neatly onto
present-day peoples, such as "Little-Russia", Lusatia, "Ruthenians" or Kharkov. In the
ten pilot entries each such mapping was made inside the entry where it came up, and is
flagged there. From the next tranche on, every label goes through one mapping table,
`sources/peoples.json`, which gives the people each label is recorded as and why. An
entry keeps the source's own word in its region text.

### Contested mappings

These rows of the table are a choice a reader may reasonably dispute. The table applies
them, and says here what the objection is:

- **"Ruthenians"** (Ralston) are recorded as Ukrainians. Some of these communities
  identify as Rusyns, a separate people.
- **The Governments of Kief and Chernigof** (Ralston) are recorded as Ukrainians, as
  part of Little-Russia. The pilot filed them as Russians, because Ralston writes of
  Russian peasants; a reader may prefer that reading for the 1870s.
- **Kharkov** (Ralston, Afanasyev) is recorded as Ukrainians, on the same rule.
- **"South-Russian"** (Ralston) is recorded as Ukrainians. The page does not say it
  means Little-Russian, and the term can also cover the Don and New Russia.
- **Afanasyev's "у насъ" ("among us")** is recorded as Russians, though the same
  passage says the belief survives chiefly in the Ukraine and White Russia.
- **Frombald's "Rätzisch" (Rascian)** is recorded as Serbs. The word named a church and
  a frontier status as much as a people.

## Reporting an error

If an entry gets something wrong, a misread source, a wrong page, a missing variant,
please open an issue at
[github.com/brac/old-wives/issues](https://github.com/brac/old-wives/issues) using the
"Entry correction" template. Name the entry and, where you can, the source and page.
